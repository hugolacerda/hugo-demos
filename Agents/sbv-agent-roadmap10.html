01: Orientation

How this actually works

The short version, no jargon. Think of it as an assembly line with stations. A folder of the client's documents goes in one end. A drafted valuation report comes out the other. Each station does exactly one job, hands its work to the next, and writes down anything it couldn't figure out.

One job each

Every agent produces one thing: a worksheet, a memo, a number, a report. Not "handles the financials." One noun. That's deliberate. An agent with one job either does it or visibly doesn't. An agent with four jobs fails at one of them quietly.

They never decide

No agent chooses a weighting or concludes what a business is worth. Those arrive from Nancy on an input sheet, and the agent applies them. The one narrow exception: Earnings & Value may propose a sourced multiple, shown with where it came from, and a person still confirms or overrides it before it's used for anything. The agent proposes, it never finalizes. That remains the only thing any agent proposes. The industry code proposal from v8 is gone at Gary's direction.

They flag, they don't fix

When something's wrong or missing (a mislabeled file, a total that doesn't add up, a market value nobody's supplied) the agent reports it and keeps going. It never quietly corrects a number or invents one to fill a gap. Everything it flags lands in one file per client, so nothing gets lost between stations.

What Nancy actually does to run it. Drop the client's documents into that client's Inputs folder, including the add-backs workbook. Tell the first agent the business name. It asks a handful of questions with clickable answers, no typing paragraphs, and sets up the folders. Then run the agents in order. Each one finishes by showing her what it made, telling her what needs her eyes, and asking whether to continue.

  • 05

Balance Sheet

Built and run Sept 10 · re-run pending on the new recast

Recasts the client's balance sheet to a seller's estimated fair market value: what it's actually worth in a sale, not what the books say it cost. Bridges from Earnings & Value's range to what the owner actually walks away with, cash-free and debt-free.

What it needs from you
  • The client's balance sheet
  • An advisor or owner estimate of real estate and fixed-asset market value, never guessed if missing
  • Earnings & Value's Range of Value for this engagement
What you get back
  • A recast table: book value, adjustment, seller's estimated market value, for every asset and liability line
  • Real estate broken out on its own, separate from other fixed assets
  • Every distinct long-term debt on its own line, never collapsed into one
  • An Enterprise Value / Net Owner Equity bridge: starting price, plus receivables minus payables, minus estimated debt payoff, plus real estate if it's part of the sale

Two treatments for fixed assets, on purpose. The going-concern price from Earnings & Value never separately prices vehicles or equipment: "we value cash flow, not vehicles." This agent's own recast does the opposite, marking them to market individually, because it's answering a different question: what does the owner walk away with in an asset sale. The two are kept deliberately separate.

Asset Method: now built. The condition you set was to look at how Peer Comps does it first, and that review is done. It confirmed Nancy's objection rather than overriding it: Peer Comps says in its own words that it is "simply allocating what assets and liabilities are included in the value/sale" rather than valuing the company's assets. So the station now produces an asset value the same way, as a two-block allocation, and the reconciliation carries it at whatever confidence Nancy gives it, which is usually zero. Worth saying plainly: a method at zero confidence still gets printed, because the goodwill figure is the estimated value less this number.

Updated Sept 19. Nancy's description of a deal — buyer pays the value, seller keeps the cash and pays off the debt — matches the Net Owner Equity walk this station already does, so that walk is confirmed and running. What is on hold is the separate Peer Comps step that adds cash and receivables into the valuation itself. With that held there is no double-count and the two stations no longer depend on each other, so both can re-run. Question 1 in the accompanying note is now a single yes or no rather than a blocker.
Caveats on Firefly, unchanged since Sept 10: no market-value estimate yet for the real estate or the fixed assets, both flagged, neither guessed. One combined depreciation account couldn't be split between the building and the vehicles. A property sub-account isn't clearly explained. A shareholder loan was zeroed out as a judgment call Nancy may want to revisit. The Sept 10 output was built on the pre-add-backs range of value and is archived; it re-runs once Earnings & Value does.

Earnings & Value Rebuilt

Base built Aug 31 · rebuilt Sept 18 against the Peer Comps walkthrough

Collapses several years of earnings into one weighted figure, applies the multiple, runs the income method, and reconciles the approaches into a single estimated value. This is the station that answers what the business is worth. It changed more than any other this week.

What it needs from you
  • Nancy's input sheet: a weight per year, summing to 100
  • The multiple, from Nancy, or her sign-off to let the agent source and propose one
  • For the income method: the discount rate components, and the revenue and margin assumptions per year
  • A confidence level per approach, for the reconciliation
What you get back
  • The weighted earnings calculation, every step shown
  • The Range of Value at all three multiples
  • The income method, with the discount rate shown as a stack rather than a single number
  • A reconciliation table carrying all three approaches to one estimated value, plus the goodwill line
The three weightings, as you laid them out
  • Across the years. Already built. One set of weights, applied once.
  • Across revenue, SDE and EBITDA. New. What gets weighted is the value each one indicates, never the multiples themselves, and the same year weights feed all three.
  • Across asset, income and market. New, and the one that was missing entirely. This is what turns two answers into one.
Two things about the income method, both from your walkthrough. It now projects five years of revenue and profitability rather than growing a single earnings figure, and the discount rate is built up from its parts and printed on the page. You were right that Peer Comps' version is not a buildup: its six risk figures are 24, 26, 28, 29, 26 and 29, which average to exactly the 27 percent it reports. It is an average of six judgment calls wearing the name of a buildup.
Also corrected while rebuilding: the terminal value was not growing the final year one more period before capitalizing. Small, invisible, and worth $266,603 on the Vesta figures. Now written out in full rather than left to the name of the formula.
Still waiting on Nancy for the income method inputs. The cash-and-receivables step is held pending question 1, and holding it is the safer default: without it the station produces the earnings-based value on its own, which is what Nancy described as the method. Everything else in this station runs today.

06: Boundaries

What the agents will not do. Not limitations to fix later. Design decisions.

Finalizing the multiple, and the weighting
Judgment

The weighting is Nancy's, full stop. A station can source and propose a multiple, but a person still says yes before it's used. An agent that could finalize it could produce a valuation, and then nobody could say whose opinion it was.

The industry code
Gary's call, Sept 15

Confirmed by a person, read by the agent. A CPA's code on a tax return is often only close, and that conversation happens internally, not in the machine.

The signature
Accountability

Every output is a draft until a person reviews the flags file and signs. Nothing leaves the machine on the agents' say-so.

Walking a client through it
The conversation

The reports exist so Gary can sit next to an owner and take them through the process. The agents build the artifact. They don't do the meeting.

One structural safeguard worth naming. When an agent doesn't know something, it stops and asks rather than filling the gap. That's why the Firefly package has "Pending" rows on the balance sheet, instead of a confident-looking number nobody chose. A visible gap is recoverable. A plausible invention that reaches a client is not.
