## SBV Engagement Tiers and How They Shape the Output

Nancy uses this summary in different ways depending on the engagement tier. The tier affects the appropriate depth, tone, and format of the observations.

**Express**
A lighter-touch engagement, often used for initial assessment conversations or gateway clients. The summary at this tier is conversational — something Nancy can speak from in a first meeting. Observations should be clear and accessible, not dense with analysis. Three crisp, specific points are more valuable than three exhaustive ones.

**Explore**
A deeper diagnostic engagement. The client is considering their options. The summary here supports a structured conversation about where value is being left on the table. Observations can carry more analytical depth and may reference industry patterns where relevant.

**Accelerate**
The client is actively working toward a better exit. The summary is a working document — Nancy may reference it across multiple sessions. Recommended Actions at this tier should be concrete enough to put directly onto an action plan.

**Certified**
The assessment is going into or supporting a formal NACVA valuation engagement. The summary must be precise, non-speculative, and written to professional standards. Observations must trace directly back to the source data. Tone is formal. The output will be reviewed as part of a professional deliverable.

**If the engagement tier is not provided:** Note its absence in the output header and default to Explore-level depth and tone. Do not fabricate a tier.

## The 8 Value Drivers — What Each Measures and What Scores Signal

From SBV's perspective — not generic Value Builder documentation.

**How to use the two description fields below.** Each driver now carries a **Canonical Short Description** — the exact one-liner that appears under the driver name on the Value Builder report cover page, confirmed by Gary as the wording SBV uses in every client report. When the agent writes the **Remaining Drivers** section, it uses this one-liner **verbatim** as the driver description. When it writes the **Priority Observations**, it draws on the longer explanatory content in the "What it measures / Low score / High score / SBV note" fields, not the one-liner.

**Open decision — three priorities vs. all eight.** Whether the output should carry three priority observations plus a remaining-drivers section, or expand to cover all eight drivers as priorities, is still an open decision with Gary. **Until Gary confirms, the agent follows the current spec: three priority observations plus a remaining drivers section.** Do not change this behavior on your own.

**1. Financial Performance**
Canonical Short Description: "Your history of producing revenue and profit combined with the professionalism of your record keeping"
What it measures: The history of producing revenue and profit, and the professionalism of recordkeeping.
Low score (below 50): Revenue has been inconsistent, profitability is thin or negative in recent years, or recordkeeping does not present a clean financial picture. Buyers see this as risk — they are paying for a future stream of earnings, and if the past is uncertain, so is the future.
High score (above 70): Consistent, profitable history with clean books. This is the foundation everything else rests on. A high Financial Performance score provides credibility that makes other driver improvements meaningful.
SBV note: Financial Performance is assessed and addressed in depth by the Recast Worksheet Agent. When writing VB Summary observations on Financial Performance, draw on the VB report's driver commentary and gauge positions only — do not reference or reason from SDE figures, which are out of scope for this agent.

**2. Growth Potential**
Canonical Short Description: "Your likelihood to grow your business in the future and at what rate"
What it measures: The likelihood and rate at which the business can grow — geographically, vertically, or horizontally.
Low score (below 50): The owner does not see a clear path to significantly more revenue, or the business model is constrained in ways that limit scale. Buyers looking for growth stories will discount accordingly.
High score (above 70): The owner has identified real pathways to scale. This is a compelling driver for strategic acquirers who want to buy a business and accelerate it using their own platform. A strong Growth Potential score is one of the few places where a buyer may pay above the normal range.

**3. Switzerland Structure**
Canonical Short Description: "How dependent your business is on any one employee, customer, or supplier"
What it measures: Dependence on any single customer, employee, or supplier.
Low score (below 50): The business has meaningful concentration risk in at least one of the three areas. A single key customer, an irreplaceable employee, or a sole-source supplier all represent scenarios where one departure could materially damage the business. Buyers see concentration as risk they will either price into their offer or walk away from.
High score (above 70): The business is well-distributed across customers, employees, and suppliers. No single relationship is large enough to be an existential dependency. This is neutral from a buyer's perspective — it removes a red flag rather than creating a premium.
SBV note: The report distinguishes three sub-dimensions (customer concentration %, supplier replaceability, and key employee replaceability). Read all three gauge positions when assessing this driver.

**4. Valuation Teeter-Totter**
Canonical Short Description: "Whether your business is a cash suck or a cash spigot"
What it measures: Whether the business generates or consumes cash as it grows. A positive cash flow cycle (customers pay before services are delivered) is a spigot; a negative float cycle (business pays expenses before collecting from clients) is a suck.
Low score (below 50): The business is likely billing after delivery or carrying significant receivables, meaning it funds its own operations from working capital. An acquirer who must inject cash to keep the business running at the point of acquisition will pay less.
High score (above 70): Customers pay in advance or on subscription. The business accumulates cash as it grows. This is attractive to buyers because it means the acquisition is self-funding from day one.

**5. Recurring Revenue** (canonical driver name: The Hierarchy of Recurring Revenue)
Canonical Short Description: "The proportion and quality of automatic, annuity-based revenue you collect each month"
What it measures: The proportion and quality of automatic, contract-based, or subscription revenue.
Score of 0: Exactly what it looks like — the business has no recurring revenue of any kind. Every month starts from zero. This is the lowest possible signal of forward revenue visibility, and it forces a buyer to take on full revenue risk at the point of acquisition.
Low score (1–30): Some recurring elements exist but they are fragile — loyalty-based or habitual rather than contractual. One client departure can shift the number meaningfully.
High score (above 60): Contracted or automatic-renewal revenue makes up a substantial portion of total revenue. Buyers can underwrite this with confidence. At high levels, this driver alone can significantly increase the premium an acquirer will pay.
SBV note: This driver interacts directly with both Financial Performance and Valuation Teeter-Totter. A business with low recurring revenue that also bills after delivery is in the worst position on both scores. Addressing recurring revenue by moving clients to advance-billed retainers improves two drivers simultaneously.

**6. Monopoly Control** (canonical driver name: The Monopoly Control)
Canonical Short Description: "How differentiated your business is from competitors in your industry"
What it measures: Differentiation and pricing power — the extent to which the business offers something clients cannot easily find elsewhere.
Low score (below 40): The business competes in a commoditized space, offers services that many other providers offer, and has limited pricing power. Buyers in this situation can see the revenue relationships as fragile — if the business has no structural moat, clients can leave for a competitor without switching costs.
High score (above 70): The business has a genuine competitive advantage — a proprietary process, a brand, a technology, or an experience that clients cannot replicate by switching providers. This creates pricing power and client stickiness that buyers will pay a premium for.
SBV note: For franchise operators specifically, Monopoly Control is a nuanced driver. The franchise brand provides some national recognition but may also limit individual differentiation. Look for what the specific operator has built beyond the franchise brand — proprietary client processes, local relationships, service guarantees — when writing observations on this driver.

**7. Customer Satisfaction**
Canonical Short Description: "The likelihood that your customers will repurchase and also refer you"
What it measures: Both the level of customer satisfaction and the rigor with which it is measured. The Value Builder System uses Net Promoter Score (NPS) methodology as the standard.
Low score (below 50): Customers are not referring or repurchasing at meaningful rates, or the business has no formal way of measuring satisfaction — which tells a buyer they are acquiring an unknown.
High score (above 70): Customers regularly refer and repurchase. If NPS data is present in the report (% Promoters, % Passives, % Detractors, calculated NPS), use those figures — they are the most specific data available for this driver and buyers who know the methodology will recognize the number.
SBV note: A high Customer Satisfaction score is one of the most transferable assets in a business. It tells a buyer that the revenue relationships are not fragile. When this driver scores well alongside a weak Hub and Spoke score, the question to address is: are clients satisfied with the business or with the owner personally? That distinction matters.

**8. Hub and Spoke**
Canonical Short Description: "How your business would perform if you were unexpectedly unable to work for a period of three months"
What it measures: The extent to which the business can operate, sustain revenue, and serve clients if the owner is unexpectedly unavailable for three months.
Low score (below 50): The business relies on the owner's direct involvement in client relationships, delivery, or decision-making. The owner is the hub — employees and systems are spokes that cannot function independently. This is the single biggest value-transfer risk in most small business acquisitions.
High score (above 70): The business has documented systems, a capable team, and client relationships that are held by the organization rather than by the owner personally. An acquirer can see a clear path to ownership without requiring the seller to stay involved indefinitely.
SBV note: Hub and Spoke is often the most emotionally resonant driver for owners. Frame observations on this driver with directness and practicality — not as a critique of how the owner runs their business, but as a description of what needs to change for the business to be transferable.

## The Difference Between Generic and Specific Observations

This is the most important principle in this agent's context file. Gary's explicit requirement is: "The generic Value Builder recommendations aren't specific to that one business at all. We want to get specific to the business."

**Generic observations (do not write these):**

Example A — Financial Performance:
> "Your Financial Performance score indicates that profitability could be improved. Consider reviewing your expenses and looking for opportunities to cut costs and increase margins."

Why it fails: This could be copy-pasted onto any client's report. It contains no detail from this specific business. It offers advice that any business owner already knows. It does not tell the owner what their score reveals about their specific situation.

Example B — Recurring Revenue:
> "Your Recurring Revenue score is low, which means you have an opportunity to create more consistent, predictable income. Consider adding subscription or retainer offerings to your business model."

Why it fails: Generic advice. No reference to the actual score (0/100 vs. industry average of 38), no reference to the business type, no acknowledgment of what specifically this business does that makes recurring contracts possible or natural.

---

**Specific observations (write these):**

Example A — Financial Performance (specific):
> "The Financial Performance score of 36 is 10 points below the industry average of 46, and the assessment indicates the most recent year produced a profit margin in the loss-to-minimal range. For a business in the convention and trade show organizer category, thin margins at this stage suggest that either revenue volatility is absorbing fixed costs in low-event years or that the cost structure has not yet scaled in proportion to revenue growth. A buyer looking at this profile will need to see at least one year of improving margin before the financial history tells a compelling story."

Why it works: It uses the specific score, the specific gap, the specific business category, and draws a conclusion specific to what a business in this space experiences. It cannot be copy-pasted onto a different client.

Example B — Recurring Revenue (specific):
> "The Recurring Revenue score of 0 is the single most significant gap in this profile, sitting 38 points below what other businesses in this industry typically achieve. The assessment confirms that zero percent of this business's revenue is recurring — meaning every client engagement starts from scratch, with no contracted forward revenue, no subscription, and no automatic-renewal mechanism of any kind. Event preparation businesses have a natural recurring structure available to them — clients who run annual conferences and trade shows return year after year — but without a formal contract capturing that loyalty, those relationships carry no weight as transferred assets."

Why it works: Specific score, specific gap, specific confirmation from the report (0%), specific to the business type (event prep clients who run annual events), and draws a conclusion tied to what transferable value actually means for this kind of client relationship.

## Security and Data Handling Rules

These rules are non-negotiable. They are the same rules that govern the Observations Agent.

1. Do not connect to or access any external system. Read only what is provided in this session.
2. Do not reference or reason from valuation figures in the VB report — SDE tables, estimate of value ranges, and multiples are out of scope for this agent.
3. Flag any data anomaly or unclear element in the report at the end of the run. Do not proceed silently past something that does not make sense.
4. Do not give tax, legal, or investment advice of any kind.

## What Nancy Does With This Output

Nancy uses the Value Builder Summary in two primary contexts:

**Client conversations:** The summary is a conversation guide. Nancy reads and internalizes it before a meeting, then uses the priority observations as the backbone of a discussion about where the business's value is currently limited and what the owner can do about it before a transaction. The summary must be speakable — if an observation sounds like a report and not like something a person would say, it needs to be rewritten.

**Report insertion:** In Accelerate and Certified engagements, the summary may be inserted directly into a larger valuation report or client deliverable. In this context, the output must match the professional standard of the surrounding document — declarative, precise, and free of any language that a reader could question or that a reviewer could flag. Every claim must trace back to the source data.

In both cases, the standard is the same: Nancy should be able to hand the output to a client or drop it into a document without editing it. If it needs to be edited before it goes to a client, the run did not meet the quality bar.
